2nd Place at Senior Design with a RAG vuln pipeline

2026-04-26 · 6 min read · capstone · AI-security · rag

The CYSE Senior Design Competition wrapped two weeks ago and I've finally had enough sleep to write this. Our team — six of us, sponsored by Avint LLC, advised by Dr. Suresh Subbaratinam, mentored by Katie Jones from Avint — took 2nd Place in the AI-Enabled Vulnerability Research, Reverse Engineering & Offensive Testing track. That's the win. This is the retro.

The problem

"Uncatalogued vulnerabilities don't wait for formal classification." That's the line we kept coming back to in the project room at 11pm. By the time a vulnerability gets a CVE number and ends up in your scanner's signature feed, adversaries have often known about it for weeks. The gap between something being discussed in the wild and something being scannable is the gap defenders lose in.

Our brief from Avint: can you build something that surfaces those discussions earlier, in a way analysts can actually use?

What we built

A Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline with four stages:

  1. Collection. Pulls unstructured security data from GitHub commits and issues, Exploit-DB drops, and the SecLists RSS feeds. Nothing exotic — everything is open-source.
  2. NLP + NER. Extracts structured entities (product, version, vector, impact verb) from messy free-text.
  3. Chunk + embed. Splits the documents, embeds them, and pushes them into a Llama 3 vector store with the named entities preserved as metadata.
  4. Scoring. A modified DREAD rubric on top of the retrieved chunks — we wanted 0–100 risk numbers instead of binary "is this a vuln," so analysts can sort.

What worked

The reasoning step. Even when the base Llama 3 model and our RAG-augmented model produced the same numeric score, our pipeline produced much better justifications — because it could cite the underlying chunks it pulled from. Analysts in our pilot review said the cited evidence was where most of the value was. We pitched it as "the score is a hint, the retrieval trail is the product."

Chunking strategy. We tried k=3, k=5, and k=10 retrievals. k=5 was the sweet spot. k=10 actually got worse because too much retrieved noise crowded out signal — a finding we ended up putting on the poster.

What I'd change

  • Eval was thin. We compared retrieved-vs-baseline scores on a holdout, but didn't have a ground-truth set of "actual CVSS scores once these eventually got catalogued." That's the eval I want to run next.
  • NER was brittle. Off-the-shelf NER misses security-specific entities (CVEs, MITRE ATT&CK techniques, software versions). A fine-tuned model would have helped a lot.
  • Collection scope creep. We tried to add Reddit + Mastodon late and it almost ate the timeline. Should have cut earlier.

Thanks

Capstone is the kind of project you only get once. I'm glad we made it count.
