The gap: vulnerabilities that haven't been catalogued yet

2026-04-10 · 5 min read · ai-security · rag · threat-intel

Here's a thing I didn't really understand until I was elbow-deep in our capstone: the CVE catalog is a lagging indicator. By the time a vulnerability has a CVE number, a CVSS score, and a row in your scanner's database, it has typically been discussed in some corner of the internet for days or weeks. The defenders see a row. The attackers saw a GitHub commit, a Mastodon post, a Discord drop.

This isn't anyone's fault. NVD does an enormous job. Triage is genuinely hard. But it means if you only rely on catalogued data, you're structurally late.

So what would early signal look like?

The free-text discussions where vulnerabilities first surface are noisy. A single useful signal is buried in thousands of false positives: someone joking, someone re-posting an old issue, someone speculating. The whole problem boils down to retrieval and ranking on unstructured threat intel.

That phrasing should sound familiar — it's exactly what RAG is good at.

Why RAG instead of fine-tuning

I asked Katie this early in the year and the answer stuck with me: "You don't want a model that knows about vulnerabilities. You want a model that can find the right vulnerability discussion when an analyst is staring at a particular product and a particular symptom."

RAG fits because:

  • The signal is constantly drifting (new repos, new feeds, new platforms). A fine-tuned model goes stale within weeks.
  • Analysts need citations, not vibes. If a score says "high," they need to see the underlying chunk.
  • You can swap collectors and embedders without retraining anything.

The hard parts (in order)

  1. NER for security entities. Off-the-shelf NER doesn't know what a CPE string is.
  2. Deduplication. A single new vulnerability shows up in 40 different repos within hours. You don't want to score the same finding 40 times.
  3. Calibration. Our DREAD scores were defensible but not well-calibrated against eventual real CVSS. That's eval work for the summer.
  4. Adversarial input. The corpus is partially adversarial — people do post fake exploits as PR. A real production system would have to think about poisoning.

What I want to write next

A focused post on k-selection in retrieval. We found k=5 outperformed k=10 on our test set, which surprised me until I realized k=10 was just stuffing the prompt with redundant chunks. There's something useful in there about the relationship between chunk diversity and retrieval depth that I haven't fully worked out yet.
